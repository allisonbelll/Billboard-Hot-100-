# HOT 100THE WEEK'S TOP SONGS

What makes a song stay on the chart?

This report analyzes 83,500 weekly Billboard Hot 100 song placements across 835 chart weeks from 2010 through 2025. The results show that chart leadership was most varied in 2020, Taylor Swift had the most song-week appearances, the largest weeks-on-chart value was 112, and the 2025 rows had the highest average chart longevity. Together, the findings separate short-term rank movement from the longer-term staying power that keeps a song visible.

Finding 01

Chart leadership was most varied in —.

Calculating the number of distinct No. 1 songs by year.

A distinct No. 1 song is counted once for each year in which it reached the top position. A larger value indicates more turnover among weekly leaders, not necessarily more total weeks spent at No. 1.

Finding 02

— had the most song-week appearances.

Calculating artist chart appearances.

This count is based on rows, so a song that remains on the chart contributes one appearance for every week it is observed. The measure captures sustained visibility in the Hot 100 universe rather than the number of distinct songs alone.

Finding 03

The largest source-reported weeks-on-chart value was —.

Calculating the longest song run in the snapshot.

The weeks-on-chart field is read from the source row and represents the song’s chart longevity at that observation. The maximum is a high-water mark in the snapshot, not a sum of all weekly rows for the song.

Finding 04

— was the most common weekly movement classification.

Calculating the movement mix.

Movement is derived from last week’s rank minus the current rank: positive values are Up, negative values are Down, zero is Unchanged, and a blank previous rank is New. The percentage compares each category with all 83,500 song-week observations.

Finding 05

The — had the strongest average rank.

Calculating average rank by decade.

Average rank is the arithmetic mean of current rank across every row in the decade. Because rank 1 is better than rank 100, the lowest average rank represents the strongest average chart position.

Finding 06

Average chart longevity was highest in —.

Calculating average weeks on chart by year.

For each year, the calculation adds the weeks-on-chart values in that year’s rows and divides by the number of rows in the year. It describes the average maturity of songs appearing in that year, not the average number of weeks a new release will eventually stay.

Finding 07

Top 10 positions accounted for — of all observations.

Calculating the share of observations in the Top 10.

The numerator is the number of rows with rank from 1 through 10; the denominator is every row in the filtered snapshot. This ratio shows how much of the dataset is concentrated in the most visible part of the chart.

Finding 08

No. 1 debuts made up — of new entries.

Calculating the share of new entries that debuted at No. 1.

A new entry is a row with no previous-week rank. The rate is calculated as new entries debuting at rank 1 divided by all new entries, so the result measures how often a song entered the chart at the top rather than climbed there later.

Data and method

How to read this report

The source is the public Historic Billboard Hot 100 Data archive. The preparation script downloads its chart archive and keeps chart dates from January 1, 2010 through December 31, 2025. One row is one song appearing on one weekly Hot 100 chart: it includes the chart date, song, artist, current rank, last-week rank, peak position, and weeks on chart. The script adds year, decade, rank change, and movement so the report can compare time periods and rank behavior.

The only rows dropped are charts outside the stated 2010–2025 date window; no song rows inside that window are manually removed, which leaves 835 weeks × 100 positions = 83,500 observations. Blank last-week rank values are retained and labeled New because they identify chart debuts. Rank change equals last-week rank minus current rank. All averages are arithmetic means, all percentages are numerator divided by denominator, and the Top 10 share uses ranks 1–10 divided by all rows. The No. 1-debut rate uses new entries as its denominator. This fixed snapshot keeps every number on the report reproducible.
